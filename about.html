About Drunken Boat

Drunken Boat was founded in 1999 by Ravi Shankar and Michael Mills with a proposition that the web is not a delivery mechanism for literature but a medium in its own right, capable of generating forms of art that print cannot approximate and a page cannot contain. Sound fields, hypertext, digital animation, locative media, interactive fiction, web art; these are not decorative additions to the literary experience but constitutive in and of themselves. For Drunken Boat, they are the point. Where John Cage's happenings dissolved the wall between composer and audience, between art and environment, Drunken Boat has spent more than a quarter century at that charged threshold where literature and technology remake each other.

One of the world's oldest electronic journals of the arts, the journal has published more than 1,000 writers and artists from across the globe, among them Norman Mailer, Lyn Hejinian, Sol LeWitt, Marcel Duchamp, Raymond Queneau, Charles Olson, Leslie Scalapino, DJ Spooky, Ocean Vuong, Natalie Diaz, and Alexandra Tolstoy. It has been featured in the Times Literary Supplement and on BBC Vietnam, nominated for a South by Southwest Webby Award, and received a special commendation from the National Aphasia Association. According to The Review Review, "Drunken Boat is, or should be, central to any discussion of literature online or online literature... a beautifully presented, carefully maintained space." The journal's reach has extended well beyond the screen: Drunken Boat has presented work and convened conversations at the Frankfurt Book Fair, the Hammer Museum in Los Angeles, the APWT conference in Manila, and has sponsored members of the Oulipo — the legendary French collective of writers and mathematicians whose constraint-based poetics have shaped experimental literature worldwide — for readings and events in New York City, among many other ventures. The journal's archives are now part of the permanent collection of the Electronic Literature Organization's The NEXT — a virtual museum, library, and preservation space for born-digital literary works — where Drunken Boat's history is maintained alongside the broader history of electronic literature as a field.

Drunken Boat has also extended its work into print when the project demands it. Its book imprint has published Reetika Vazirani's Radha Says, a posthumous collection painstakingly reconstructed from manuscript; Lisa Russ Spaar's Hide-and-Seek Muse, her essential annotations of contemporary poetry; and Collier Nogues's The Ground I Stand on Is Not My Ground, selected by Forrest Gander for the journal's inaugural first-book prize, a hybrid of erasure and digital art that exists simultaneously as object and website. Meridian: The APWT Drunken Boat Anthology of New Writing gathered 37 voices from across Asia and its diaspora; Union marked the journal's 15th anniversary alongside 50 years of Singaporean literature; Three Indian Poets brought together a stunning trio of subcontinental voices. Most recently, Bhutan, co-authored by Sudeep Sen and Ravi Shankar and co-published by Nigeria’s Fortunate Traveler, maps a Himalayan kingdom in lyric and image.

Drunken Boat has not always had smooth sailing — the name alone invites turbulence — and like any institution that matters, it has attracted those who would rather rename it than build something of their own. It has outlasted them all. What began in 1999 as a conviction about what literature could do online remains, exactly that: a conviction and a journal.

Drunken Boat is a 501(c)(3) nonprofit organization. It has no university subsidy, no institutional patron, no advertising. It depends entirely on the support of readers who believe that literature — in all its born-digital, cross-media, radically open forms — is worth sustaining. If that describes you, please consider making a donation.

What Drunken Boat has always believed and still believes is that tribalism reduces us and that the diversity in these pages is not a posture but constitutive and non-negotiable. The canon is not closed. The web, for all its wreckage and noise, remains a space where a poem, an animation, a sound field, a work of hypertext can reach anyone, anywhere, for free. That still feels like the most radical idea we know.
