Editor’s Introduction

Issue 26

Welcome to Issue 26 of Drunken Boat! This issue is organized around three folios—Statelessness, A.K. Ramanujan, and Born-Digital. They each ask a version of the same question: how do we persist? What remains of a people when the state withdraws recognition? What remains of an iconic poet translated out of his own tongue, censored out of a university curriculum, too little known in the West? What remains to foundational works of art when the software that once ran them disappears? This issue is our answer, an act of preservation against political, institutional, and technological erasure. In each case, something essential has been made to disappear, or been threatened with disappearance, and the artists and writers and archivists and editors here refuse to let that happen.

Statelessness

Walking the Tufts campus in the fall of 2025, I found myself moved by the scatter of tents on the quad, Palestinian flags in the New England wind. They recalled the fervor of campus protests during Vietnam and I felt heartened to see the activism. Just months earlier, Rümeysa Öztürk, a Turkish doctoral student at Tufts on her way to break her Ramadan fast, had been surrounded by six masked plainclothes ICE agents outside her Somerville apartment and taken away. Her apparent offense? Co-authoring an op-ed in the Tufts Daily calling for the University to divest from companies with direct or indirect ties to Israel.

What followed was purely Orwellian, not just on my campus but around the country. Criticism of Israeli state policy got transmuted into the smear of antisemitism, the Department of Defense turned into the Department of War, free speech had become evidence of terrorism. Watching this happen where I taught, I felt the entire frame of the conversation shift. To speak of Gaza solely as a question of Palestinian rights was insufficient since it arrived pre-entangled with the shadow of Hamas, the long wound of the Nakba, the Balfour Declaration, the competing claims that cause reasonable people to talk past each other, when they were not shouting. What if we set that frame aside? What if we simply looked at the way we looked—often too late or not closely enough—at Serbia and Kosovo, at Rwanda, at the Yazidis? At the actual human beings? At the homes reduced to rubble, the families who fled, the dead children whose names we will never know? What if we reframed this political issue as a human rights one? That shift led me, almost inevitably, to a larger question: not Palestine specifically, but statehood itself.

What does it mean to exist without the recognition of the state? To be present in the world, making art, burying the dead, yet be told you were legally invisible? It is the lived reality of the Roma, the Rohingya, the Bidoon, the Uyghurs, the Kurds, the Palestinians, and dozens of other peoples whose belonging is contested or denied. The lens of statelessness illuminates Gaza more honestly than much existing discourse because it refuses the partisan frame and insists on the irreducible fact of human life. That is how this folio came to be.

What we did not anticipate was the sheer diversity of forms that statelessness would call forth. From the Basque Country, Rikardo Arregi, a two-time winner of Spain’s Critics Prize writing in Euskara, one of Europe’s most imperiled languages, maps the fragile reefs between identity and belonging. From the Hmong diaspora, Ka Vang, born on a CIA military base in Long Cheng, Laos, raised in Thai refugee camps, fuses magic realism with refugee girlhood and Hmong oral tradition. From Kurdistan, Walid Siti’s paintings, sculptures, and installations, held by the British Museum, the V&A, and the Met, transform the landscape of Iraqi Kurdistan into an abstract grammar of power and fragility, made by a man in political exile since 1984. Tsering Wangmo Dhompa, the first Tibetan woman to publish poetry in English, writes what she calls the “dark matter” of exile and her fellow Tibetan contributors extend that witness into film and activism. From the Palestinians — Ahmed Hmeedat, Mohamed Harb, Osama Nazzal, Rashid Abdelhamad, Tarzan Nasser, and Yahia Lababidi — come poems, visual art, film and cartoons that insist, against every force arrayed against that insistence, on the continued reality of a people and a place. Though none of them could speak for a displaced people and are sometimes in the diaspora, Tabish Khair represents the Biharis; Ebrahim Alipoor, Savas Boyraz, and Shadi Harouni the Kurds; Muyesser Abdulehed and Tahir Izgil the Uyghurs; Amaia Gabantxo the Roma. For the response to erasure is never silence. It is, always and stubbornly, more art.

A.K. Ramanujan

In graduate school, Ramanujan was a hero of mine. Poet, translator, folklorist, philologist. When I found The Striders (1966) at the Strand, I was hooked. He was one of the few South Asian poets I had heard of, and through him I discovered Nissim Ezekiel, Arun Kolatkar, Dilip Chitre, R. Parthasarathy, and so many other giants hidden in plain sight. What serendipity, then, that as an Indo-American Arts Council board member I was able to invite his son Krishna Ramanujan to New York. Krishna had just co-published Soma with Guillermo Rodriguez, Director of the Casa de la India in Valladolid. It is a volume built around a remarkable discovery of unpublished poems from Ramanujan’s archives, written in a more incantatory register than his earlier work, obsessed with the identity of the mysterious Vedic plant. The book gathers these Soma poems alongside essays by Krishna and Rodriguez, a reprinted 1968 essay by Wendy Doniger that directly shaped Ramanujan’s thinking, and a 1982 interview conducted by poet K. Ayyappa Paniker in Chicago. Together they illuminate both a private obsession and a volatile period in Western Indology whose influence persists today.

That event became a conversation, and that conversation became the archive before you now: unseen poem drafts, photographs, archival video, jottings on index cards, an interview with Studs Terkel. This is the residue of a mind that never stopped crossing languages and traditions. A MacArthur Fellow and recipient of India’s Padma Sri, Ramanujan spent most of his career at the University of Chicago revealing that alongside Sanskrit high culture, India possessed thousands of years of rich folk tradition in languages like Tamil. Saul Bellow was astonished by how deeply he had absorbed British and American poetry; Girish Karnad wrote a play from a Kannada myth he had simply heard Ramanujan tell. And yet in the West, outside academic South Asian studies, he remains scandalously underread.

The afterlife of his work has not been tranquil. His 1987 essay “Three Hundred Ramayanas” demonstrated how the Rama story had accumulated countless variations across 2,500 years and dozens of languages. Placed on Delhi University’s history syllabus in 2006, it was removed in 2011 after right-wing Hindu groups (who had already vandalized the history department over it) claimed its scholarly attention to heterodox versions was offensive. What the controversy revealed was the magnitude of the threat; here was a scholar claiming no tradition is singular, no text original, no telling final. The suppression of Ramanujan’s essay, with its insistence on plurality over purity, echoes everything this issue of Drunken Boat is concerned with. To insist that there is only one Ramayana is not so different from insisting that there is only one people with a legitimate claim to a land. Both are acts of erasure that serve power. Both are challenged by the very existence of this archive.

Born-Digital

Finally, since Drunken Boat’s founding in 1999, we have published digital artists at whatever the frontier happened to be — Shockwave, Flash, Gnash, Lightspark, interactive hypertext. Much of that work was made in software since rendered obsolete, swallowed by platform updates and discontinued plugins. A generation of electronic literature has been disappearing and not because it is being burned or banned but simply unable to run. That’s where The NEXT comes in. Envisioned as a combination museum, library, and preservation space, The NEXT keeps born-digital art alive and accessible by deploying tools like Ruffle, a Flash emulator written in Rust, to give the obsolete a pulse. The entire Drunken Boat archive, all 25 issues over 26 years, is being hosted at The NEXT and work we published at the millennium’s turn will hopefully be viewable again by scholars who weren’t born when we first published it. The NEXT is generously supported by the Electronic Literature Lab at Washington State University Vancouver and the Electronic Literature Organization. If you’re considering a charitable gift and believe the digital record of human imagination deserves preservation as surely as any manuscript in any library, please consider supporting them.

This folio would not exist without the design team at the NEXT and our Managing Editor Lili Newberry, who solicited and curated all this work endemic to the web, work the medium itself made possible and which only the medium can sustain. Drunken Boat has never wanted to replicate the paradigm of the page but instead to take advantage of digital storytelling’s interactivity, temporality, and spatial freedom. In some instances, we are publishing documentation of immersive installations and in other cases, the actual works themselves, but always towards a sense of what is possible when the human imagination utilizes digital technology.

Sasha Stiles, a first-generation Kalmyk-American poet, Harvard and Oxford graduate, contributes A LIVING POEM, an infinite generative text co-created with her AI alter ego Technelegy, recently exhibited on a 25-foot screen in MoMA’s lobby, rewriting itself every 60 minutes. Peter Burr, a Guggenheim Fellow whose video installations have shown at Documenta 14, the Whitney, and the Centre Pompidou, offers Count Zero which is a digital sculpture in ninety-nine layers of monochromatic husk, hovering between the video game dungeon and immersive cinema. Ashley Zelinskie’s Wanderer extends her practice of translating human heritage into forms legible to future machines. These are among just a few of the digital revelations in the folio.

With that, Drunken Boat is proud to present Issue 26. We continue to publish books and have just partnered with the Nigerian publisher Fortunate Traveller to release Bhutan, which weaves an essay I wrote about spending a little time in that mysterious mountainous Himalayan kingdom together with stunning photographs and poems by Sudeep Sen. Since its inception, Drunken Boat has remained a 501(c)(3) nonprofit that could not exist without your support. If this issue moves you, please tell someone, share a piece that stays with you, donate. The infrastructure of independent literary publishing is more fragile than ever, and the artists in these pages and the venues that showcase them deserve our support.

We are living through a difficult time. For art, for truth, for the very idea that human lives have equal value regardless of what passport they carry or what state recognizes their existence. Drunken Boat has always believed that literature and art are one of the few places where that equality is not argued for but simply enacted, one voice at a time. Keep engaging. Keep creating. Keep insisting on the beauty of what you know to be true.

— Ravi Shankar, Editor-in-Chief, Drunken Boat
