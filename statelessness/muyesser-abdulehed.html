The Weakness of Language

In my mother tongue, I breathe my mother’s earth;
each day, I dwell again in her homeland.
My dreams endure within its syllables,
my waking self learns its own name.
Its beauty dazzles my eyes,
its wisdom dissolves upon my tongue.
If those who made me homeless truly knew,
they would seize my tongue, not my land—
they would chain my speech.

In the language of the enemy, I return their oppression,
unlock their square-script cages,
freeing those bound to my soul.
My vision blurs;
above me, the gallows hangs in threat.
Yet in this language I declare:
I will not die with death.
In this language, I loose my final arrow.

In the language of my faith, my soul finds rest.
I become a bird—rising into a clear sky;
I become rain—falling from the clouds.
When snow descends from the realm of miracles,
I scatter myself, piece by trembling piece.
I no longer know my mind, my heart, my face—
I cry out: I am not a form.
In this language, I am given glad tidings:
death is not terror, but arrival.
In this language, I utter my final testimony,
and turn my heart into a shield against bullets.

I live in the language of the land I inhabit—
this language echoes what my mother once said.
This language summons my origins, questions my roots,
sends greetings from the Taurus Mountains
to my sorrowful Tengri Tagh.
I grow distant within nearness,
I come loose within my bonds.
My hopes are steeped in grief,
my cries take on the cadence of this tongue.

I speak to the world in the world’s own language.
My whys are without end.
I do not trust it—
it does not see me; why does it not look?
It does not feel me; why does it not hear?
I break open the dam within me—
in this language, I will flood.
I will kindle the fire inside me,
become a volcano—
and erupt.
I want to tear this language from my tongue—
a word leaves me in it,
and the world no longer understands its own language.

In what language does dawn rise each day?
In what language does the sun set?
Among all tongues, my complaints,
my burnings, my fractures, my ruin,
dissolve into the simple saying: a bad dream.
I will live upon the dark soil,
and I will reach the place of the mute.

Returning to the Fire

I will return to him,
Return to them!
My eyes can't sleep anyway,
I see a night through all my days…
Down my fingers my stars are seeping away.
I will sit beside him and close my eyes,
Let the dark dungeons have their nightmares!
Let my dreams laugh by their side…

Never mind, let my hands be tied,
As long as it's not my heart being burned again.
Whips, please slash my face,
My strangled soul cannot take anymore,
The noose is about to break!

Don't cry if you hear the news of my “passing”!
Say the cold, the hunger, the thirst,
And the crisis of identity,
Set me free.
Cast the stones from your heart,
Don't give me another thought.

It is my hatred for sobbing that will buy the ticket,
It is my hatred for weeping that will pull my luggage,
It is the mourning, which seized my heart,
That will release my veins and relax my fears,
When the black bag over my head shouts, “Ürümchi,”
It is just my burial shroud running for my mum's tomb….
What ease to go back like this!
With cruelty to grant my wishes,
Before the final death.
When you hear the strange news “He died,”
Say, “He is not dead,”
How can one die in the soil of one's homeland?
His only duty now is to smile,
Clinging to the bright white earth that has been his home all the while.

ABOUT THE ARTIST

Muyesser Abdul'ehed (Hendan) is a poet, writer, editor and educator. A native of Ghulja in the north of East Turkistan, Muyesser completed a medical degree at Beijing University, followed by a master's in University of Malaya. After relocating to Turkey in 2013, she resolved to focus on writing and teaching Uyghur language. Her debut novel, Farewell to the Sun, is the first work of fiction to focus on the internment camps in East Turkistan.

She established the organization Ayhan Education, whose purpose is to foster the Uyghur language in diaspora communities. Their work includes publishing a Uyghur language magazine written by children, aimed at their peers. The magazine is called Totqulaq (Four Leaf Clover).

Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, Muyesser taught in-person language lessons in Istanbul, but since then she funded Uyghur Online Language School. The school is now expanded with more than 20 teacher and reached over 400 students.

She was on the list of the BBC's 100 Women announced on 23 November 2020.
