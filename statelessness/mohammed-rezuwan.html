Rohingya Art

Mohammed Rezuwan

Naeema

"Naeema, where are you?"

Her mother's voice cut through the fog.

It was just before dawn. The village lay still beneath a blanket of mist—until the gunshots of rifles and AK-47 shattered the silence.

"Naeema!"

This time, desperation. Naeema woke instantly. Her heart pounded with bitterness, burning smell filled the air. She didn't think of that.

She ran. Barefoot, she raced past the water well, toward the jungle far away from the village, leaving her parents in the sea of tears. At five years old, she knew these paths well. If nothing stopped her, she could reach the dense forest in less than a minute.

Gunfire cracked again—closer and closer.

Naeema was a Rohingya.

She did not fully understand the word, only that it meant she did not belong and her existence doesn't exist, at least not in the eyes of the military giants with guns. The government called her people illegal aliens, even though they had been living on that land for generations and the soils of that land mixed with their blood.

Villages were burned. Families shattered.

And, the whole community erased.

Months earlier, a family had come through Sodari Fara, a village from very far away.

"They don't burn the villages anymore," one of the family members had said quietly. "They erased the existence."

No one understood.

"They bulldozed," he continued. "When we returned, there was nothing. No houses. Not even ashes. Just burned lands. As if we had never lived there."

Silence filled the room.

"Now I wonder," he added, "do we Rohingya even exist?" "Bring the mat here!" and he fell down.

The voice froze Naeema.

Through the fog, she saw soldiers, trucks, and vans filled with human bodies. She ducked behind a banyan tree and slipped into its hollow trunk, pressing herself into darkness. The jungle was close. Her family would be waiting. But the soldiers stood between her and everything she saw. Then came the sound, shivering the whole village.

A soft hiss.

The smell followed—sweet at first, then sharp, burning her throat and eyes.

She covered her mouth, trembling. Through blurred vision, she saw the impossible.

The houses were fading. Walls falling down. Roofs flying, and the ground swallowing the tears and blood.

Her home—gone. Her dream—shattered.

Erased.

As if it had never existed.

Tears streamed down her face. A small family of rabbits huddled beside her.

She met the gaze of one. And suddenly, everything went still.

"You will live, little one." The voice was calm. Certain. Darkness took her.

When Naeema awoke, the world was silent.

She sat up slowly.

Where Sodari Fara had once stood, there was now only a wide, empty field.

No houses. No people. No trace.

Her chest tightened.

If the land itself could forget her village, who would remember her people?

Years passed. The jungle became rivers. The rivers became graveyards and borders and led to the camps in Bangladesh.

The world's largest camp.

Rows of wooden shelters stretched endlessly, filled with people who carried their homes only in memory. Everything is said to be temporary. But temporary became years.

Naeema grew.

She learned what it meant to live without a country—not through words, but through absence.

No identity. No recognition.

No place that claimed her.

Stateless.

One evening, hungry and tired, Naeema walked along the edge of the camp fenced with barbed wire. She stumbled over something. An old woman lay in the dust.

"I'm sorry," Naeema said, kneeling. "Are you okay?"

The woman looked up. It was Nargis.

"I want fish curry," she whispered. "Like my mother used to make."

Her voice trembled.

"That is all I want before I die."

Naeema felt something shift inside her.

She had nothing. Still, she said softly, "It's here."

Nargis blinked.

Naeema placed a metal plate in front of her and covered it with her shawl.

"Are you ready?" she asked.

"Yes," Nargis whispered.

Naeema lifted the cloth.

Rice. Fish curry. Warm. Real. Traditional Rohingya meal.

The smell filled the air around.

Nargis gasped, then laughed and cried as she ate.

Naeema stared.

She didn't understand. But she knew this was real.

The next day, Nargis called her again. "You did it," she said. "Do it again."

"I don't know how," Naeema replied. "You do," Nargis said gently. "You feel."

Naeema shook her head. "Feeling won't give us a country."

Nargis smiled faintly.

"No," she said. "But it gives us something they cannot take."

She leaned closer. "You felt my hunger—and you gave me more than food. You gave me home."

Naeema looked away.

"What good is that?" she whispered. "We are still nothing to the world."

Nargis's voice softened. "That is what statelessness tries to do," she said. "It tries to make you believe you are nothing."

She paused.

"But you are not nothing. You remember. You feel. You carry others inside you. That cannot be erased."

That night, Nargis died beneath her tree. No family came. No prayers were spoken.

Only silence.

Days later, Naeema stood by the water, staring at her reflection.

The surface trembled. For a moment, she thought she saw someone else looking back—stronger, steadier.

A presence stirred beside her.

She turned.

A tiger stood there—silent, powerful. She should have been afraid.

But she wasn't. Slowly, she placed her hand on its head. It lowered its head—not in submission, but in recognition. Memories returned.

The rabbits. The voice. The village that had vanished.

The hunger. The anger. And the truth growing within her. They could erase her home.

They could deny her identity. They could call her illegal alien on her own land.

But they could not erase what she carried inside her.

Naeema closed her eyes. Somewhere beyond borders, beyond camps, beyond names denied to her people—they still existed.

Unseen. Unwanted.

But alive. Stateless.

And yet—

Not without memory. Not without dignity. Not without power.

For the first time, Naeema understood:

Statelessness is not just the loss of land. It is the attempt to erase a people.

And to resist it is not only to survive—

but to remember, to feel,

and to remain.

ABOUT THE ARTIST

Mohammed Rezuwan is a Rohingya folklorist, storyteller, and human rights advocate. He is the author of Rohingya Folktales Book: Stories from Arakan, as Told by Rohingya Refugees and co-author of All Walls Collapse, a fiction magazine published by Comma Press (UK). Rezuwan examines how folktales and storytelling preserve language and culture across generations, even in the face of exile, and decades of state-sponsored genocide faced by the Rohingya people, through his work on Rohingya folktales passed down orally for centuries which might otherwise have been lost. His work on Rohingya folktales bridges the Rohingya community and international audiences, while providing young Rohingya children growing up in refugee camps and the diaspora with space to learn about their culture, folktales and language.

He is the Founder and CEO of Global Rohingya Future (GRF), a refugee-led organization promoting education, human rights advocacy, anti-human trafficking and cultural documentation in the world's largest refugee camp. Beyond his literary work, Rezuwan has served as a fixer, interpreter, educator, and researcher with international organizations, including Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF). His storytelling and photography have been featured in The Guardian, CNN, VOA, and the South China Morning Post. Now living in Ottawa, Rezuwan continues to amplify Rohingya voices and affirm that culture is not left behind in displacement but carried forward—generation by generation—even in the most fragile spaces of exile.
