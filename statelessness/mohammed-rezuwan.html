Ka Vang

HMONG

INcorrect

The French did not arrive as villains.
They arrived with ironed uniforms
With cake.
With constellation maps that pressed mountains flat
as dough.
My grandfather said
the water turned first—
metal on the tongue,
coins floating on top of currents.
They renamed rivers
until the fish reshaped
mouths around French
so precisely,
surrendering
Nyab zoo into Bonjour
Cua daj, cua dub into Orage.
Being stepped on
does not require polished boots.
A ledger will do.
A wet thumb of holy water
extinguishing incense
declaring your ancestors
incorrect.
Children bent at the spine
not from age
but from taxes.
We were jungle pigs.
Once feral
now
necks ringed.
Counted.
Elephants did not kowtow.
So we stitched silver into collars—
not ornament.
Exit.
If a name is taken
and a number given—
when it is spoken,
do you turn?
We carried dried corn in our teeth,
bows across our backs,
hunting for Shee Yee’s silhouette—
No one teaches the Hmong
how to flee beautifully.
You run
with your grandmother’s bones
stitched into sacks of rice.
You sweeten what cannot be swallowed.
Red for the quiet wound.
White for the lie called salvation.
Blue for the sky
that witnessed
and kept its distance.
If sugar is survival
and survival must be served—
when you open your mouth,
what nation
melts on your tongue?
Glossary:
Tricolor (Naab Vaam): a popular Hmong dessert made with Tapioca pearls, syrup and coconut milk
Nyab zoo: Hello in Hmong
Cua daj, cua dub: Thunderstorm in Hmong
Shee Yee: First Hmong Shaman.

Classified

They did not arrive with cake.
They arrived whistling,
strumming guitars.

Metal Pegasus
circling the tips of the mountains,
bellies full of guns, bibles
and powdered milk.

They said the word “liberty”
as if it were a rope.
We were told
to climb.

Men in mirrored sunglasses,
unfurled maps
that did not flatten us—
they lifted us,
pointed,
said:
you know these hills.
You are small.
You are fast.
You are loyal.

We were given radios
that crackled in a borrowed tongue
given rifles
longer than the boys who carried them,
given a war without a name.

If you are taught
to fire for someone else’s flag—
are you also being schooled
in how to belong to it?

The mountains remembered
what the French forgot:
metal sinks.
We were made
to disappear in the Mekong.

Across rivers swollen with silt.
Across borders that vanished by dawn.
Sib ntsib dua.
Sib ntsib dua.
I will light incense
in a bowl of rice
on the other side.

In the refugee camps,
barbed wire did not need
to be cruel.
Hunger did the counting.
Milk powder.
Plastic tarps.
Numbers again.

We boarded planes
with bones still ringing
from Plains of Jar battles.

The ocean did not ask questions.
On arrival,
the sky was bigger.
The roads straighter.
The supermarkets bright
as confession.

We were told
this was winning.

If you fight for a country
you were not born in,
what do you call yourself?
Refugee.
Ally.
Asset.
Resettled.

When the sky speaks English,
and you answer—
who hears you

Glossary:
Sib ntsib dua: Literally “until we meet again.” Used as goodbye in Hmong, grounded in the belief
that separation is temporary and reunion is certain.

Naturalized

We did not arrive in helicopters.
We arrived with paperwork.

Forms in triplicate.
Numbers that matched
until they didn’t.

We were given project housing lawns.
Mailboxes.
Flags to unfurl and hang.
They said the word citizen
as if it were written in permanent marker.

Spring rain, then winter snow—
not ash,
not dust from bombed hills,
but clean.

In grocery aisles
our carts moved between milk
and cereal mascots
smiling with perfect teeth.

Our elders learned to say Mee-Da-No.
Our children answered McDonald’s.

We learned the pledge.
We stood for the anthem.
Before we learned
which neighbors would not
return our waves.

When Covid came
it came with new names.
Foreign.
Carrier.
Contagious.

In playground gravel
my children were reassigned
a country not their own.

Spit on sidewalks.
Fists in parking lots.
Old men—former warriors—meeting pavement.
Aunties walking faster in daylight.

If a country calls you model,
does it also mean silent?

Masks over mouths
that had already practiced
obedience.

If citizenship is printed
but belonging is spoken—
when they call your number,
do you turn?

And if you turn—
who writes it down?

ABOUT THE ARTIST

Ka Vang is a Hmong American writer born in Laos and raised in Minnesota. Her work moves between memory and migration, exploring belonging, inheritance, and the quiet negotiations of citizenship. She is the author of Shoua and the Northern Lights Dragon and numerous essays and stories featured in anthologies and literary publication. Currently, she is a contributing columnist for the Minnesota Star Tribune. She lives in Minnesota.
