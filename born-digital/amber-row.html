Amber Row

Jenna Caravello

Amber Row is an experimental video game about grief. Formatted like an open-world, third-person explorer, the act of collecting and managing an inventory of virtual objects represents the labor involved in grieving a loved one.

Inspired by Sabine Harrer's book, Games and Bereavement, and by explorations of Active Worlds (a defunct online video game circa 1995), Amber Row uses an allegory for loss as its central motivation. The story begins when the player drops unwittingly into the virtual world, obliterating the body of a previous avatar, assuming her general silhouette, and scattering her memories as collectible objects. The central mechanic of collecting these objects relates “grinding” in games (or, working hard to achieve merit) to the work involved in mourning death, loss of self, and loss of community, exploring grief as a place to dwell in comfortable liminality.

Finding the few collectible objects that do not belong to the original avatar and returning them to characters in the game world reveals snippets of insight into the origins of the game, but slowly destroys both the world and the player's avatar. As the player gathers more objects, their body distorts. When the player attempts to return objects, the world itself begins to fall apart. There is no score, only a process of disassociation through exhaustive effort and repetition in a virtual world where the most critical agency may be to put the controller down. When every memory object in the game has been collected, a final confrontation ejects the player from the world, in the hope that they may focus instead on collecting memories of their own.

Amber Row is a narrative auto-fiction that reflects on the loss of Caravello's mother and the comforting stasis of video game worlds. The game is scalable and is playable in galleries as an installation with a custom controller.

About the Artist

Jenna Caravello is an artist working in animation, computer games, and installation. Across these forms she tells deeply personal stories, often from non-human perspectives, that are somber and eerie, but colorful and tactile. These works explore modern love, especially—physical and digital impermanence, the identity politics of digital avatars, longing, liminality, and relationships across online forums.

Caravello received an MFA from CalArts and a BFA from SAIC. She teaches animation and experimental games in the department of Design Media Arts at UCLA.
